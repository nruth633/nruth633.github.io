What it is

A three-wheel robot that holds itself upright by driving its wheels against the direction it's falling. The IMU reports attitude, the control loop turns that into a motor command, and the whole thing runs on a board I designed and assembled myself.

It was built across several iterations. Rather than overwriting each revision, every one is kept in the repository — so the progression from the first two-wheel board through to the current version stays visible and reviewable.

How it's put together

The encoder-free variant

One branch of the project drops the wheel encoders entirely and estimates attitude from the IMU alone. That removes a sensor and its wiring, but it also removes the velocity feedback the loop was leaning on — so the balance controller has to do more with less. Keeping it alongside the encoder version makes the tradeoff concrete instead of theoretical.

The board

The control board puts everything the loop needs on one PCB, built around an STM32F401RBT6 on a 16 MHz crystal. A TB6612FNG drives the motors; the MPU6050, MT6701 magnetic encoders, ESP8266 radio, and motor leads all land on their own connectors rather than being soldered to the chassis. Power comes in over USB-C — behind a PTC fuse, CC resistors, and a USBLC6 ESD array — and splits through LD1117 regulators into 5 V and 3.3 V rails, each with its own indicator LED. A WS2812B gives the firmware one addressable status light, and a 4-pin SWD header keeps the debugger connected.

The switch labelled 5 V selector is the detail worth pointing at. It cuts the motor rail while leaving the logic powered from USB, so the board can be flashed and stepped through on the bench without a balancing robot trying to drive itself off the desk. That's a bench-ergonomics decision more than an electrical one, and it's the kind of thing you only add after being bitten once.

Populated by hand

The boards came back bare and every surface-mount part went on by hand — the passives, both regulators, the TB6612, the crystal, and the STM32 itself in a 64-pin quad flat pack. Nothing on this board was placed by a machine.

That constraint shows up in the layout, not just the assembly. Parts are spaced for an iron and tweezers rather than packed as tightly as the design rules allow, and anything that takes mechanical load — the USB-C receptacle, the JST connectors, the headers — is through-hole rather than sitting on surface-mount pads that peel.

Building it yourself

The firmware folders are STM32CubeIDE projects — open with File → Open Projects from File System, then build. Build output is gitignored, so a clean checkout compiles from source rather than shipping stale binaries. The KiCad projects open with KiCad 7 or later; the footprint cache and rolling backup folders are excluded and regenerate on first open.
