Designing for the machine, not the fab house

Most PCB projects end by uploading gerbers to a manufacturer who handles the physical realities. This one was milled in house on an LPKF ProtoMat S64, which moves those realities back onto the designer.

The mill needs its own output set — front copper, back copper, drill file, and edge cuts — and it drills the holes you specify rather than the holes it assumes you meant. So the drill sizes have to be decided deliberately: 0.7 mm for vias, 1.5 mm for component leads. Get those wrong and the board comes off the bed with holes your parts won't fit through.

The footprint problem

Stock KiCad footprints carry drill sizes chosen for commercial fabrication, which didn't match what the mill needed. Rather than hand-editing every pad, the fix was to build a custom footprint library: modify one part properly — a resistor, say — with the correct drill specification, then assign that footprint to every part of the same type across the board.

It's a small workflow decision that stops a tedious, error-prone edit from being repeated dozens of times, and it means the next board milled on the same machine starts from a library that already matches it.
