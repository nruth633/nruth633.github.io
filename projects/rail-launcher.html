Electromagnetic Rail Launcher

  • ~330 J
  • 225 V
  • 13,200 µF
  • Boost converter
Source
11.1 V, 2200 mAh LiPo (rechargeable, by design)
Converter
DC boost module — fuse, op-amps, transformer, trim pot
Storage
13,200 µF — six 250 V / 2200 µF electrolytics in parallel
Energy
E = ½CV² ≈ 0.5 × 0.0132 × 225² ≈ 330 J
Inrush limit
12 × 220 Ω in series (2.64 kΩ) → 85 mA charge current
Blocking
600 V diode ahead of the resistor bank
Rails
Two solid aluminium rods; steel BB armature
Housing
3D printed

The brief I set myself

Get as much stored energy per dollar as possible, use rails rather than a coil, make it rechargeable, make it safe to operate, and make it actually work. The rails are the easy part. Everything interesting is in getting 330 joules into a capacitor bank from a battery and releasing it without destroying the circuit that charged it.

How it works

Current runs up one rail, through the armature, and back down the other. The two rails carry current in opposite directions, so the magnetic field between them and the current through the projectile produce a Lorentz force that drives the armature down the rails. Force scales with current, so the whole design is about producing a very large, very brief current pulse — which is what a capacitor bank is for.

Protecting the charger

A discharged capacitor bank looks like a short circuit. Connect a boost converter directly to 13,200 µF sitting at 0 V and the only thing limiting current is parasitic resistance — which is enough to kill the converter on the very first charge.

So the charge path goes through 2.64 kΩ, holding the current to about 85 mA at 225 V. The detail I like here is that it's twelve 220 Ω resistors in series rather than one 2.6 kΩ part. The resistance is the same either way; the difference is power handling. Splitting the string spreads the dissipation across twelve packages instead of cooking one, which is the difference between a resistor that survives repeated charge cycles and one that chars.

The 600 V diode sits ahead of that bank so the discharge can't travel backwards into the converter when the capacitors dump.

Where it got difficult

Bonding the rails to the supply wires was harder than expected — aluminium is unpleasant to solder, and the joint has to carry the full discharge current without becoming the weak point. Rail spacing was equally fiddly: too loose and the armature loses contact, too tight and it binds instead of accelerating.

Two failure modes dominate this kind of launcher. Rail erosion — the arc and plasma at the contact point eat the rail surface, so the bore degrades with every shot. And the armature welding itself to the rails: if the projectile starts from rest, the current arrives before it's moving and fuses it in place. The fix is to give it an initial velocity so it's already sliding when the bank dumps.

On safety. 330 J at 225 V is genuinely dangerous, and a capacitor bank stays dangerous long after the supply is disconnected. "Make it safe" was an explicit design objective, not an afterthought — hence the enclosed 3D-printed housing, the fused converter, and a deliberate charge and discharge procedure.
