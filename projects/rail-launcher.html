What it does

A rail-type electromagnetic launcher. Current flowing up one rail, through a projectile, and back down the other produces a Lorentz force that accelerates the projectile along the rails. The engineering problem isn't the rails — it's getting a few hundred joules into a capacitor bank from a battery, and then releasing it all at once without destroying the circuit that charged it.

Charging stage

A DC boost converter steps the 11.1 V LiPo up to roughly 225 V, charging six 250 V / 2200 µF electrolytics wired in parallel for a combined 13,200 µF. At 225 V that bank holds on the order of 330 joules — consistent with ½CV², which is the number worth quoting because it's what actually goes into the shot.

Running the capacitors at 225 V against a 250 V rating leaves headroom rather than sitting at the edge of the part's spec, which matters for electrolytics that degrade under repeated hard cycling.

Protecting the charger

An empty capacitor bank looks like a short circuit. Connect a boost converter straight to 13,200 µF at 0 V and the inrush current is limited only by parasitic resistance — enough to destroy the converter on the first charge cycle.

A 2.6 kΩ resistor network limits that inrush to something the charging stage survives, and a 600 V blocking diode keeps the discharge from travelling backwards into the converter when the bank dumps. Both are cheap parts standing between a working circuit and a destroyed one.

On safety. A charged 13,200 µF bank at 225 V is genuinely dangerous and stays dangerous after the supply is disconnected. This was built and operated with bleeder discharge, insulated tooling, and verification that the bank was down before touching anything.
