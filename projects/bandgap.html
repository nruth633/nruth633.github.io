Measuring the Silicon Bandgap from One Transistor

  • Eg = 1.31 eV
  • k = 1.56 × 10⁻²³ J/K
  • R² = 0.97
  • −1 to 75 °C

What I set out to do

Pull two fundamental physical constants — the silicon bandgap and Boltzmann's constant — out of a single transistor, using a 9 V battery, three NPN devices, a switch, a few resistors and a multimeter. Then combine the two results to build a voltage that barely moves with temperature: the bandgap reference hiding inside almost every integrated circuit.

Why it works

A forward-biased junction follows VBE = (kT/q)·ln(I/IS). The saturation current IS rises steeply with temperature because it carries the bandgap inside it as exp(−Eg/kT), and that growth dominates — so VBE falls almost linearly as the device heats, by roughly 2 mV per kelvin in silicon. Follow that straight line down to 0 K and every thermal term vanishes, leaving an intercept equal to Eg/q. A temperature sweep therefore hands you the bandgap.

For Boltzmann's constant, run the same transistor at two different currents and subtract. The awkward, device-specific IS is identical in both readings so it cancels completely, leaving ΔVBE = (kT/q)·ln(I₁/I₂). That difference grows linearly with absolute temperature, and since the current ratio is known, it solves for k.

The measurement

The device under test is a diode-connected NPN — collector tied to base. Its current is set by an all-NPN current mirror running as a sink, so the bias holds steady even as the device heats. A switch selects either a 10 k or 100 k reference resistor, giving two currents about 10:1 apart for the ΔVBE half of the experiment.

The design decision I'm happiest with is keeping the mirror out of the bath at room temperature. If the current source heated along with the device, the current would drift with temperature and I'd be measuring two variables at once instead of one. Eight baths, roughly −1 °C to 75 °C, three minutes to settle each.

Throwing data away, on purpose

Every 1 k reading went in the bin: at that current the 9 V battery sagged badly and the readings slid while I watched them. I also dropped three 10 k points as outliers because they showed a higher voltage at a higher temperature, which the physics forbids — VBE only falls as things warm up. That left five clean points for the bandgap fit and four paired points for Boltzmann.

Discarding measurements needs a reason beyond "it didn't fit." Here the reason was physical: one set had a known supply problem, the other violated the monotonic behaviour the device must obey.

Results

Quantity Measured Accepted Error
VBE slope −2.16 mV/K (R² = 0.97) ≈ −2 mV/K —
Silicon bandgap 1.31 eV 1.12 eV at room temperature, ≈1.17 eV at 0 K +17 % / +12 %
Boltzmann's constant 1.56 × 10⁻²³ J/K 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K +13 %

Building the reference

The last step adds the falling VBE to the rising ΔVBE, weighted by about 9.8 — chosen precisely so the two slopes cancel. The sum comes out flat at roughly 1.31 V across the whole temperature range. That is a bandgap reference, the stable voltage block built into nearly every chip, and it lands on the same 1.31 V as the extrapolated intercept, which closes the loop back to where the project started.

Where the error comes from

Both constants came out high, for two reasons I can point at. The real VBE curve bends slightly rather than being perfectly straight, so fitting a line and pushing it all the way to 0 K overshoots the true intercept. And my bath temperatures weren't perfectly settled — one cold point even read below a warmer one, which is visible scatter.

That scatter hurts Boltzmann more than the bandgap, because ΔVBE is a ~60 mV difference between two much larger readings, so a small error in either gets amplified. Tighter temperature control and a wider sweep would pull both closer.

Landing within 13–17 % of textbook values, from a 9 V battery and a handheld multimeter, with the discrepancy fully accounted for, is a result I'll take.
